ABSTRACT

In the last few years, digital constitutionalism has emerged as a novel, alternative, Internet Governance approach aiming at ordering and limiting the exercise of power by both states and private operators, as well as at promoting people’s control over digital technology development.

Although digital constitutionalism is encountering growing popularity, the prevalent non-binding nature of its initiatives, the discrepancy between the jurisdictional border and transnational digital processes, and the technological embedment of governance mechanisms, have hindered the effectiveness of its impact.

This paper identifies epistemic communities as a crucial factor for the constitutionalization of the digital technologies since they have the necessary technical expertise and policy commitment to create norms and standards at transnational level, informing the policy choices of both state and non-state actors.

In order to illustrate this nexus, the article performs an in-depth investigation on the case of the European Commission High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence (HLEG-AI). The analysis revealed that the HLEG-AI effectively gathered an epistemic community strongly committed with fundamental rights promotion and capable to influence the following policy-making activities of the European Commission, as well as of other non-state stakeholders, whose involvement is essential in order to embed digital constitutionalism principles within the design of AI systems.

1. Introduction

The development of the Internet and other digital technologies has raised several constitutional concerns. The more that digital technologies become crucial to everyday life, the more that they both empower people and endanger an increasing number of fundamental rights.

Internet governance scholars have detected two main approaches that have characterized Internet-related policy-making since its early stage (Goldsmith & Wu, 2006; Hofmann, 2007; Kurbalija, 2016; Mueller, 2010). The neoliberal approach promoted private self-regulation to foster technological innovation and development; however, this approach also favored the concentration of almost unrestricted power in the hands of a modest minority of corporations. Instead, the sovereignist mode claimed states’ authority in cyberspace to safeguard national interests, but ultimately, this led instead to censorship and surveillance. Neither has multistakeholderism, a compromise between the two approaches based on dialogue and cooperation among different stakeholders, proven capable of addressing democratic legitimacy (Hofmann, 2016; Palladino & Santaniello, 2020) and human rights standards compliance issues (Jørgensen, 2011; Zalmieriute, 2019).

Activists, civil society, and international organizations have attempted to tether Internet governance to human rights and the rule of law since the late 1990s.¹ Only in recent years have scholars framed these arguments as an alternative Internet governance approach under the label “digital constitutionalism, [...] a common term to connect a constellation of initiatives that have sought to articulate a set of political rights, governance norms, and limitations on the exercise of power on the Internet” (Redeker et al., 2018, p. 304, see also; Celeste, 2019; Suzor, 2010; Teubner, 2004; Zalmieriute, 2015).

This work argues that epistemic communities have the potential to play a crucial role in the constitutionalization of the Internet and in digital technologies’ governance. Epistemic communities are networks of “professionals with recognized expertise and competence in a particular domain and an authoritative claim to policy-relevant knowledge” (Haas, 1992, p. 3) that are intended to exert a notable influence on policy-making, especially during the early stages of novel, highly complex, technical issues (Cross, 2013; Radaelli, 1999; Zito, 2001). Epistemic communities are known for “elucidating cause-and-effect relationships and providing advice on the likely results of various courses of action; shedding light on the complex interlinkages between issues” (Dunlop, 2012, p. 230), and framing decision-makers’ and other actors’ understanding of a phenomenon, and in doing so, “they provide one of the major channels through which overarching regime principles, norms and rules are articulated for the international community and are disseminated internationally” (Haas, 2014, p. 32).

Epistemic communities appear to be particularly relevant within the domain of Internet governance. Indeed, since the creation of the Internet, the shared beliefs and visions of a cohesive technical community have strongly shaped Internet policy-making (Drake, 2004; Hofmann, 2007). Even today, the possibility of influencing the development of an information policy regime in a transnational landscape relies on the ability to manage the creation and dissemination of accepted knowledge and beliefs (Braman, 2004; Cogburn, 2017). In reality, the technical community concerned with Internet standards and protocols has always been reluctant to consider the social and political implications of digital technologies (Hoffman 2007, Malcolm 2008). Only in recent years, human rights concerns are emerging within operational and standard setting organizations, such as ICANN and IEFT (Cath & Floridi, 2017; Milan & Oever, 2017), but as Corinne Cath’s research in this volume testifies, the enduring tensions between past and emerging trends undermines digital constitutionalism claims in Internet protocol standard-setting organizations. However, the expansion of Internet governance boundaries beyond network protocols (Kurbalija, 2016) toward more interdisciplinary issues, where human rights concerns have more political salience (i.e., algorithms, big data, privacy, and surveillance), has opened the door to more engaged epistemic communities.

This paper illustrates the role of the epistemic community in the constitutionalization of the Internet and in digital technology governance through an in-depth analysis of the work of the EU High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence (HLEG-AI), established by the European Commission (EC) in 2018.

There are several reasons why the HLEG-AI is a particularly suitable example for demonstrating an epistemic community’s promotion of a digital constitutionalism framework.

The first reason concerns the issue at hand: artificial intelligence² (AI) is a novel and complex technical phenomenon still in its early stages, raising the most intense debate about the impact of new technologies on society.

Despite their potential benefits, AI systems raise concerns that range from privacy and data protection to discrimination, manipulation, misinformation, endangerment of democratic institutions and civil liberties, and effects on jobs and rights on the workplace (Brownword and Yeung 2018; Latzer & Just, 2020; O’Neil, 2016; Turner, 2019).

Not by chance, recent years have witnessed a flourish of AI ethical initiatives (Berkman Center 2020), an occurrence that has led scholars to wonder if this “ethification” corresponds to “ethical washing” practices that mask the usual self-governance paradigm, and under what conditions this trend could lead to effective promotion of fundamental rights (Floridi, 2019; van Dijck & Casiraghi, 2020; Yeung et al., 2019).

Second, knowledge and expertise have always been pivotal in European Union policy-making practices (Majone, 1996; Radaelli, 1999), especially for the EC. The EC specializes in regulatory rather than redistributive policies, and as noted, “knowledge, rather than budget, is the critical resource in regulatory policy-making” (Radaelli 1999, p. 759). Experts can also provide “information that is scarce and valuable to member states,” thereby transcending “the bias of information imbued with national interests” (Gornitzka & Sverdrup, 2011, p. 52) and contributing to the integration process. Moreover, expert advice can foster the Commission’s ability to anticipate objections and concerns from the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union, thus increasing the chances of acceptance for a Commission proposal (Tsebelis and Yataganas 2002).

Third, the European Commission is fully aware of both AI’s potential and the concerns that this technology raises. ## 2. Digital constitutionalism and epistemic communities: A necessary nexus?

Although digital constitutionalism is increasing in popularity, leading to some relevant outcomes,³ some factors undermine its effective affirmation.

First, very often, digital constitutionalism initiatives consist of non-binding documents drafted by civil society organizations, multistakeholder fora, private actors, or international organizations.

As noted, “the general lack of binding instruments among interventions in digital constitutionalism is a common source of skepticism regarding the effectiveness of their impact” and “exposes digital constitutionalism to the risk of being turned into a rhetorical device” (Padovani & Santaniello, 2018, p. 4). Although we can witness over the past few years the increasing number of legislative initiatives following a digital constitutionalism approach (Santaniello et al., 2018), the translation of digital constitutionalism principles and values into binding legislative acts remains rather limited and usually a cumbersome phenomenon.⁴

Even when digital constitutionalism principles are translated into laws, more challenging difficulties for the affirmation of common rules ordering and limiting the power in Internet governance stem from the transnational nature of the digital processes. Internet governance is often described in terms of “peer governance” (Benkler, 2006) or “network governance” (Mueller, 2010; van Eaten and Mueller 2013), the latter of which denotes “environments with low formalization, heterogeneous organizational forms, large numbers of actors, and massively distributed authority and decision-making power” (van Eaten and Mueller 2013, p. 730), and involves myriad, often transborder interactions among “owners, operators, developers, and users” (Rosenau & Czempiel, 1992, p. 18; see also Hofmann et al., 2017). This view has been challenged by the reconsideration of states’ capabilities to control digital technologies in light of increasingly common national “cyber-sovereignty” strategies (Goldsmith & Wu, 2006; Nye, 2011), and by the immense concentration of power in a handful of giant corporations (Green 2018, Zuboff, 2019). In the end, Internet governance could be described as a “patchworks of partly complementary, partly competing regulatory elements in the form of legal rules and ordinances, mandatory and voluntary technical standards and protocols, international and national contracts and agreements, and informal codes of conduct and netiquette” (Feick and Werle 2010: 525). Even so, the transborder and distributed nature of digital technologies operations and interactions, with the core technical and public functions that private companies carry out (DeNardis, 2014), challenges the effectiveness of domestic constitutional constraints and international human rights law.

Furthermore, regardless of whether governments, private corporations, or unintentional interactions shaped software and hardware architectures, these architectures are a “law” in themselves (Lessig, 2006) in the sense that they control and regulate people’s behavior by tracing and by allowing or inhibiting certain courses of action. Digital technology design could then affect humans’ integrity and autonomy, and this “invisible work” taking place within day-to-day, operational, yet politically salient practices (DeNardis & Musiani, 2016; Stars 1999) could make fundamental rights violations hard to perceive and then be remedied (e.g., the international community has become aware of NSA mass surveillance programs only because of an insider’s disclosures; see Laprise, 2016). Whether consciously or unconsciously, “the decisions embedded within protocols embed values and reflect the socioeconomic and political interests of protocol developers” (Denardis 2013: 10).

It follows that standard-setting bodies “are increasingly becoming arenas for tussles over value-sensitive design, and the moral as well as legal responsibility of technologists to protect human rights-by-design” (Cath & Floridi, 2017, p. 450). Lack of awareness and responsibility among technical operators about the negative externalities and dangers to the integrity of individuals, communities, and institutions related to digital development could foster fundamental rights violations. As Lessig (2006:6) noted, “this code presents the greatest threat to liberal or libertarian ideals, as well as their greatest promise. We can build, or architect, or code cyberspace to protect values that we believe are fundamental, or we can build, or architect, or code cyberspace to allow those values to disappear”.

From the above, it should be clear that, for digital constitutionalism to be a workable and effective governance approach, it cannot be confined to the legal system nor to aspirational civil society initiatives, but it should act at the interplay between the design of digital technologies and the production processes of social norms, and it should involve policy-makers, developers, and users. In this regard, epistemic communities can play a crucial role in creating, spreading, and institutionalizing a shared set of norms, rules, and procedures, between both ‘legal’ and ‘technical’ regulators.

necessary scientists) sharing a specific belief system, which comprises the following:

1) Principled and casual beliefs, which provide a value-based rationale for behavior and analytic reasons and explanations that elucidate the relationships among events, and then also between certain policy options and their outcomes, in terms of causes and consequences;
2) Common notions of validity, which provide “intersubjective, internally defined criteria for validating knowledge and allow community members to differentiate between warranted and unwarranted claims about states of the world and policies to change those states” (Haas, 2014, p. 30); and
3) A common policy enterprise that is “a set of common practices associated with a set of problems to which their professional competence is directed, presumably out of the conviction that human welfare will be enhanced as a consequence” (Haas, 1992, p. 3).

Epistemic communities can be understood as discursive policy networks held together by the production and reproduction of specific policy discourses based on validated knowledge. Past professional or educational backgrounds could favor an ideational alignment (Schneiker & Joachim, 2018), and collaboration among experts, common affiliations, or attendance to the same set of organizations, conferences, or initiatives could be all important indicators of the sharing of validated knowledge and then of an epistemic community at work (Cross, 2013; Haas, 1992).

Haas’s framework, with its specific combination of belief and consensual knowledge, offers both criteria to distinguish epistemic communities from other kinds of actors involved in policy-making and an explanation of their influence. Epistemic communities provide framing and narratives of a perceived reality validated through well-established methodologies. Sometimes, as Hajer’s studies on environmentalism testify, the institutionalization of a specific measurement technique constitutes both the validation criteria capable of shedding light on phenomena and one of the key practices to address the problem (Hajer, 1995). Validated knowledge is also important inasmuch as it characterizes the epistemic community’s views as rational and impartial, above political conflicts and bargaining (Haas, 2014). In this regard, the internal cohesion of the epistemic community and the absence of relevant competing groups also play a crucial role (Cross, 2013). The more that an epistemic community monopolizes a certain policy domain (at least in some specific policy venue) by speaking with one voice, the more that its claims appear “objective,” thus enhancing their credibility and legitimacy.

Epistemic communities’ frameworks and policies are particularly valuable under the condition of uncertainty (Haas, 1992; Radaelli, 1999). The more uncertainty there is, the more that actors “will search for information to inform their choice,” enabling the “epistemic community to provide information that excludes or enhances different alternatives” (Zito, 2001, p. 588), that is frames and narratives selecting and connecting particular aspects of a phenomenon, shaping its definition, highlighting causal interpretation, suggesting moral evaluations, and prescribing the response to the phenomenon to pursue shared social values (Entman, 1993; Jones et al., 2014; Stone, 2002). Complex, technical, and novel issues, such as digital technologies, foster uncertainty and, as consequence, epistemic communities’ influence (Cross, 2013; Peterson & Bomberg, 1999) because, in these cases, other actors have neither a clear and comprehensive perception of the phenomenon nor consolidated beliefs and preferences.

Thus, frames by epistemic communities combining technical expertise with ethical and human rights concerns at the early stage of emerging technologies have the potential to shape technological development according with digital constitutionalism views, informing both operative practices and regulation.

It is worth noting that the belief system that Haas described allows for distinguishing epistemic communities from other groups involved in policy-making (Haas, 1992), in particular from advocacy groups, which have a common policy enterprise but not casual beliefs and a validation notions (Haas, 2014). Advocacy groups are perceived more frequently as strategic political actors and “lack the broader social deference necessary to command influence” (Haas, 2014, p. 28). Furthermore, Haas (2014: 40) noted that “advocacy groups promote prohibitionary regimes,” while epistemic communities promote “regulatory regimes” based on social learning. This difference could help to explain why the efforts of human rights activists and civil society associations within standard setting and operational arenas such as IETF and ICANN encounter so much resistance and are sometimes alleged to threaten innovation and the efficiency of the Internet by the technical community (see Cath in this volume, Milan & Oever, 2017, Mueller and Badiei, 2019). At the same time, the difference from advocacy groups, and between prohibitionary/regulatory approaches sheds light on the potential of epistemic communities for the embedment of digital constitutionalism principles within digital technologies design.

Epistemic communities could then overcome the challenges posed to digital constitutionalism by the transnational nature of digital technologies, providing the transnational level with a common set of legitimate norms, rules, and procedures. Carayannis et al. (2012: 135) highlighted how “epistemic communities are relevant to the consideration of a global model of regulation in that they are formed by international technical expertise” because they can create “uniform systems of rules and norms that will form the basis of a global policymaking agenda” (Carayannis et al. (2012 132). According to Cogburn (2017: 28), epistemic communities might be critical in international regime formation (Padovani et al., 2010), favoring the “convergence of multistakeholder (e.g., governments, private sector, and civil society) expectations around the principles, values, norms, rules and decision-making procedures in a specific area, or interrelated areas, of international affairs”.

To our purpose, it is worth noting that epistemic communities not only influence governments but also other non-state actors with decision-making power. Epistemic communities “are at the forefront of recognized trends towards transnational governance, and they are a major means by which knowledge translates into power [...] it is clear that epistemic communities not only seek to persuade states, but also a wide variety of non-state actors. They are not only underpinning specific government policies, but also shaping governance more broadly” (Cross, 2013, pp. 138–9).

Another important point that the literature stresses is the capability of an epistemic community to build alliances with other distinct kinds of organizations such as advocacy networks, communities of practices, and professional associations (Dunlop, 2012; Sebenius, 1992).

In particular, the involvement of the people concerned with day-to-day digital technologies development and operations is deemed crucial for the affirmation of a digital constitutionalism prospective. As noted, “the technical community will not only be best placed but will have the sole ability to protect human rights standards [...] precisely because they are the only community able to see the human rights issues that have been hard-wired into the very way in which the Internet operates” (Liddicoat & Doria, 2012, p. 15). Recently, Gasser and Schmitt (2020) pointed out how professional ethics developed within trade organizations, professional associations and companies are a relevant source of norms production for AI governance. The authors also reported cases in which “open protests by employees of leading AI companies—such as Microsoft and Google [...] led to the development of company-specific AI principles.” (Gasser & Schmitt, 2020, p. 146). Epistemic communities with a strong commitment toward digital rights could provide these professionals with a consistent normative framework and practical guidance on how to embed human rights and ethical standards within operational routine and organizational practices.

In the end, epistemic communities could act as hubs, spreading digital constitutionalism standards and principles and influencing both public authority lawmaking and technological shaping by non-state actors.

### 3. Research questions, data, and methods

The case of the European Commission’s High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence (HLEG-AI) appears to be a strong candidate for illustrating and further investigating the relationship between digital constitutionalism and epistemic communities, as described in the previous paragraph.

The Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology (DGCONNECT) selected the members of the group through an open call launched in spring 2018 on the basis of their “competence and experience, including at European and/or international level, in artificial intelligence and its applications, including other areas of relevance for artificial intelligence such as technological, commercial/business and other aspects such as law, fundamental rights, ethics, consumer protection, behavioral science, privacy and personal data protection, social science, philosophy, psychology and neuroscience, arts and media, economics, and labour market economics (all applicants).”⁵

The expert group was entrusted to the following responsibilities: 1) “propose to the Commission AI ethics guidelines, covering issues such as fairness, safety, transparency, the future of work, democracy and more broadly the impact on the application of the Charter of Fundamental Rights, including privacy and personal data protection, dignity, consumer protection and non-discrimination”; 2) “advise the Commission on next steps addressing AI-related mid to long-term challenges and opportunities”; and 3) “support the Commission on further engagement and outreach mechanisms.” For this last purpose, the Commission also created a broad multi-stakeholder platform, the European AI Alliance, to gather input, exchange views, and develop and implement common measures to encourage the development and use of AI. In particular, the expert group was responsible for gathering input from the AI Alliance on its recommendations. In short, the HLEG-AI is composed of experts from different backgrounds, manages the impact of new technology on human rights, and was asked to provide guidance for other actors and support their engagement, all crucial features of the epistemic community–digital constitutionalism nexus.

However, this work does not consider the HLEG-AI as an epistemic community in itself but rather as an advisory body that gathers potential members from one or more pre-existing epistemic communities. This difference becomes more evident, and interesting, if we examine the use that the EC does of expert groups and the actual composition of the HLEG-AI. As Metz summarized, the EC could pursue a “problem solving” use of experts, to gain information and evaluate options; as well as “a politically substantiating use, so as to gain expert support for a predefined position against other actors”; or a “political consensus-building use, so as to generate consensus among relevant actors in prospect of formal decision making” (Metz, 2015, p. 10).

To these different uses correspond different kinds of expert groups: problem-solving is performed by “specialist committees,” small groups of experts in the field entrusted with narrow mandates; substantial support is entrusted to “high-level groups” based on the visibility and reputation of their members; and “consensus building” relies on large “negotiating arenas” composed of “representatives of European peak-level societal associations or national governments” (Metz, 2015, pp. 40–5).

In the end, the DGCONNECT appointed 52 members on the basis of their expertise, of which 19 individuals were appointed in their personal capacity (Type A members); 1 was appointed to represent a common interest that stakeholders shared, namely, the European Digital SME Alliance (Type B members); 30 members represented organizations, including academic institutions, companies, consumer organizations, trade unions, and civil society interest groups (Type C members); 2 members were representatives from other public entities, namely, the European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights and the European Economic and Social Committee (Type E members). The HLEG-AI appears to be a hybrid of the three, as if the European Commission intended to bring members of aligned epistemic communities within a broader multistakeholder arena to develop, legitimize, and build consensus around its AI strategy.

In the light of these considerations, the research questions that this paper addresses are as follows:

This question entails the following:

1.1) Investigate the affiliation and backgrounds of HLEG-AI's members to detect indicators of previously existing epistemic communities.
1.2) Explore if the members of the HLEG-AI share a set of normative and causal beliefs, an agreed-upon notion of validity, and a common policy enterprise? Did this shared belief system represent a digital constitutionalism framework?

With the purpose of verifying if the HLEG-AI gathered or gave rise to one or more epistemic communities, biographical notes and curricula of the members of the expert group have been retrieved from their personal pages or institutional websites and have been employed in an affiliation network analysis; which is a particular kind of social network analysis designed to explore two-mode networks, namely, networks composed of two different sets of entities—in this case, members and organizations (Borgatti et al., 2013; Carrington et al., 2005). Data on co-affiliation are usually employed as a "proxy of social relation" on the assumption that "co-affiliation provides the conditions for the development of social ties of various kinds," and then data on "social proximity" could grant insight into "social similarity" (Borgatti and Halgin 2014: 420).

After retracing current and past affiliations of each member of the HLEG-AI and constructing a member-organization matrix, I extracted both a member-network (in which members are tied by co-affiliations to the same organizations), and an organization-network (in which a tie between two different organizations corresponds to a common member) and related graphs and statistics through the software Gephi.

In this case, affiliation network analysis is supposed to provide information on three different aspects relevant to this inquiry:

i) If the HLEG-AI's members have some common professional or educational background through which they could have acquired similar ideas, approaches, and cultural norms;
ii) If the HLEG-AI's members effectively worked together in organizations or initiatives, which could indicate an epistemic community in action; and
iii) The number and the variety of organizations to which each member of the HLEG-AI is related, which could provide some insight about the potential for the spread and dissemination of the whole group. This point is particularly relevant to RQ3.

To investigate if, and to what extent, the members of the HLEG-AI effectively shared common beliefs, I realized a second two-mode network that related actors with concepts. This kind of analysis could be conceived as a simplified version of a discursive network analysis, as Leifeld (2017) described.

To this purpose, I collected texts (e.g., public statements, articles, blog posts, and reports) drafted by the HLEG-AI members concerning AI and, if possible, its ethical and fundamental rights implications. I strove to select materials released prior to the establishment of the HLEG-AI and when this was not possible, I resorted to items published afterward. In some cases, regarding members appointed as representatives of an organization, I included statements and position papers released on behalf of the organization in the analysis even if they could not be surely attributed to the individual member joining the HLEG-AI. I hand-coded key concepts contained in this textual corpus via the software NVIVO, and I then built a matrix of members-concepts and performed network analysis via the software Gephi.

The analysis then allowed to map the arguments and beliefs of the HLEG-AI members, to identify the most shared concepts and ideas, and to ascertain the HLEG-AI members grouped around them; this made it possible in the end to verify if HLEG-AI members shared an epistemic community belief system.

The results of this latter analysis are also useful to answering RQ2 and to exploring to what extent the outputs of the HLEG-AI represent a convergence of expectations around a core set of digital constitutionalism principles and values. Comparison of the content of HLEG-AI outputs with the socio-conceptual network of its members could indeed provide insights into what extent divergences among members (if any) have been resolved, thereby leading to a higher synthesis, and how this process could signal a move towards digital constitutionalism. Unfortunately, transcripts of the HLEG-AI meetings were not made available to the public, so it was not possible to retrace the discussion and discursive interactions within the HLEG-AI that led to the outputs.

With regard to RQ3, as mentioned above, the results of affiliation analysis could provide some insight into the potential for the spread and dissemination of the HLEG-AI. Furthermore, to investigate the impact of the HLEG-AI on European policy-making, I analyzed EU institutions and member states' policy documents concerning AI, as well as the policies of non-state actors (such as private firms and research centers, especially those involved in the European AI Alliance initiative), that were released after the HLEG-AI submitted its output. I scrutinized these documents to determine if they refer to HLEG-AI outputs and to assess what content has been accepted most.

## 4. Results and discussion

### 4.1. Background and affiliations of HLEG-AI members

Affiliation analysis identified the existence of two different components within the HLEG-AI, that is, two different “set of nodes in which every node can reach every other by some path” (Borgatti et al., 2013, p. 26). In this case, these components entailed a web of shared or overlapping membership with a set of organizations (Fig. 1, Table 1). The first component gathers 29 members, and the second 7, while the remaining 16 members of the HLEG-AI have no co-affiliations with the other members of the expert group.

Fig. 1 shows also that the first component could be further broken down into three clusters based on the density of their interconnection.⁶ Cluster 1 has a clear tech-academic profile; most of its members belong to the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) and share subgroups or initiatives such as the IEEE Global Initiative on Ethics of Autonomous and Intelligent Systems or the IEEE Robotics and Automation Society. Further, some of them share training or teaching experience in tech-focused universities. The vast majority of members in Cluster 2 had or have some professional experience in IBM and have taken part in advisory bodies of national European governments, and two members of this cluster are also members of the Future of Life Institute. Members of Cluster 3, instead, share overlapping affiliations with some Europe-wide organizations; this cluster gathers current and former members of EU agencies, committees, expert groups (such as College of Europe, European University Institute, and Airbus), and European trade and business associations. Some of these members further share training or teaching experience at the Max Planck Society’s institutes. All the members of the second component (Cluster 4) belong to private firms and are scarcely related to other stakeholder groups. The members of the HLEG-AI who do not have co-affiliations with other members constitute the most variegated group (“No Co-affiliation”) and show the highest concentration of civil society associations.

These findings suggest that the HLEG-AI could have gathered members from one or more pre-existing epistemic communities that were developed around specific professional, academic, and technical organizations.

Further evidence of the existence of already extant epistemic communities within the HLEG-AI stems from the fact that many of its members co-authored, or subscribed to, relevant texts in the field of AIs ethics and principles, such as the IEEE’s “Ethically Aligned Design,” the Future of Life Institute’s “Asilomar AI Principles,” or AI4People’s “Ethical Framework for a Good AI Society.” Members of the HLEG-AI then not only have a shared background in terms of training and professional experiences but also effectively cooperated on some initiatives.

Although most of the authors of the IEEE’s “Ethically Aligned Design” belong to Cluster 1 and some of the authors of the Future of Life Institute’s “Asilomar AI Principles” are in Cluster 2, all the co-authored documents have been drafted or subscribed to by members of different clusters.

These findings suggest that at least the first component could mostly constitute the manifestation of a unique epistemic community developed around several poles and held together by members and initiatives that act as bridges among different parts of the network.⁷ A network composed for the most part of academic or technical organizations, or of individuals with prior academic experience, capable of finding a place within governmental advisory bodies, research departments of private firms, or multistakeholder policy-making arenas (see Table 1).

Among HLEG-AI members, there is a strong consensus among the key principles, which should drive the development of a human-centric AI. In particular, most of them emphasized a triad of strictly related principles—transparency, explicability and accountability—that are deemed to play a pivotal role in creating a trustworthy environment for AI. Within an AI context, transparency entails the provision of information about “the data used, the fact that a user interface is not human, as well as [...] the identity of those that trained the system, and basic information on how the system was trained” (Renda 2019: 53). Explicability could be understood as a sub-dimension of transparency that focuses on the explanation of the decision-making process that the AI carries out. Both transparency and explicability are a pre-condition for accountability, which in this context is mainly concerned with the identification of responsibility in the case of harmful output and the related remedy to procedures.

Another cluster of related principles includes fairness, equality, non-discrimination, and solidarity. All these principles are concerned with the capability of AI systems to produce exclusion and inequalities in at least three different dimensions. First, it is possible that AI systems are designed to embed bias, or are trained with biased data, in such a way that they systematically damage certain groups of people. Second, even when they are not biased, AI systems could exclude vulnerable and marginalized groups from the access to basic services (e.g., health insurance). Third, in a broad sense, the equal distribution of AI benefits society overall.

Privacy and data protection are also often mentioned, mainly in reference to individuals’ access to and control over their data and digital identity as well as to the guarantee that personal data are properly secured. This last aspect necessitates the consideration of another shared principle: safety, which refers to the safeguarding of human integrity and prevention of harm through technical robustness to prevent malfunctioning, errors, or hacking.

The policy enterprise of the many HLEG-AI members also entails a geopolitical purpose. From a European perspective, ethics is considered crucial to the creation of AI standards that safeguard EU values and sovereignty. It could represent the added-value of a European way to AI, capable to generate the trust necessary for AI development and full acceptance by society at large and make EU a global leader in this field.

Lastly, the policy enterprise of this epistemic community also includes the development of procedures and instruments to embed the abovementioned values and principles effectively within the design of an AI system, such as via impact and risk assessment, auditing, and tests. In acknowledgement that this process is still in its infancy, most members of the HLEG-AI have sought funding for and investments into this research.

HLEG-AI members have also shared **notions of validity**. Despite, as seen above, HLEG-AI members have converged upon a relatively short list of ethical principles, a widely held belief is that there is not one ethical methodology or tradition to be preferred, meaning that any list of principles must not be considered definitive (the AI4People initiative discusses a “living document”), and these principles could conflict with each other. Thus, each ethical consideration should be placed in its context and driven by evidence-based approach and scientific research. This point also implies a stance for ethics as an ongoing process or a mindset that should permeate each AI-related activity rather than as a fixed reference system.

A subset of these beliefs assumes that the impact of AI on humans and society could be quantified through proper measurement and metrics and that these metrics could be embedded within AI design or used to monitor, evaluate, and modify AI systems. The “Ethical Guidelines” appears to reflect the epistemic community belief system as described above. The guidelines indeed draw on the “human-centric” narrative, thereby transcending both a cyber-enthusiastic and a cyber-apocalyptic approach; their declared aim is to “set out a framework for achieving trustworthy AI” and to foster AI development by “seeking to maximise the benefits of AI systems while at the same time preventing and minimising their risks.” As a whole, the guidelines recall all the principles and validation criteria mentioned above as well as geopolitical considerations and the belief about the necessity to embed principles within socio-technical design.

Through the work of the HLEG-AI, the commitment toward digital constitutionalism principles, already present in many of its members, reaches an even broader consensus and a clearer definition, thereby becoming foundational in the HLEG-AI view. Further, the principle of the embedment of digital constitutionalism principles within socio-technical design finds in the experience of the HLEG-AI a pivotal moment of development. The guideline states: “We believe in an approach to AI ethics based on the fundamental rights enshrined in the EU Treaties, the EU Charter and international human rights law. Respect for fundamental rights, within a framework of democracy and the rule of law, provides the most promising foundations for identifying abstract ethical principles and values, which can be operationalized in the context of AI.”

Moreover, the guidelines clarify the relationship among fundamental rights, ethical principles, and law or regulation.

The trustworthy approach is grounded in fundamental rights and composed of three components: lawful AI, ethical AI, and robust AI. As notes, “Understood as legally enforceable rights, fundamental rights therefore fall under the first component of Trustworthy AI (lawful AI), which safeguards compliance with the law. Understood as the rights of everyone, rooted in the inherent moral status of human beings, they also underpin the second component of Trustworthy AI (ethical AI).” The third component, robust AI, refers to the expectation that AI systems perform their activities in a safe, secure, and reliable manner, from both a technical and societal perspective.

From fundamental rights stem four ethical imperatives for the AI environment (respect for human autonomy, prevention of harm, fairness, and explicability), translated into “seven key requirements that AI systems should implement and meet throughout their entire life cycle” by “developers, deployers and end-users, as well as the broader society”: human agency and oversight, technical robustness and safety, privacy and data governance, transparency, diversity, non-discrimination and fairness, environmental and societal well-being, and accountability. The ethical approach is then conceived as a complement to, not a substitute for, law and regulation, which could overcome the difficulties encountered by law in terms of jurisdiction, technological pace, and specificity.

Unlike most other AI ethical codes, the HLEG-AI’s “Ethical Guidelines” also introduces a set of technical and non-technical methods to implement ethical requirements and provide a pilot version of a concrete assessment list aimed at operationalizing trustworthy AI and assessing the fulfillment of ethical requirements.

## 5. Conclusions

This study assumed that epistemic communities play a crucial role in the constitutionalization of the Internet and digital technologies because they have the necessary technical expertise and policy commitment to provide the transnational level of governance with a common set of norms, rules, and procedures, informing state lawmaking, professional ethics, and organization practices of non-state actors. To illustrate this nexus, an in-depth investigation on the case of the EC’s HLEG-AI has been performed.

The analysis revealed that the HLEG-AI effectively gathered an epistemic community that shared normative and causal beliefs, notions of validity, and a policy enterprise. This epistemic community has proven to be strongly committed to fundamental rights, conceived as instruments both to unleash the potential and to limit the negative impact on the integrity and autonomy of individuals and communities of AI systems.

The inquiry has also identified how the work of this epistemic community was able to influence the following policy-making activities of the European Commission and member states as well as to raise awareness and accountability of the non-state

The HLEG-AI appears to be a particularly favorable case to observe an epistemic community shaping policy-making because the EC selected members of already existing epistemic communities in line with its values and strategies. The Commission has ostensibly used the reputations of epistemic communities' members and validated knowledge to develop an already established policy strategy and legitimize this strategy in front of other veto players in the EU system (Parliament and Council) and the broader public. However, the other direction of this relationship and the autonomous capacity of epistemic communities to exert influence should not be undermined. The HLEG-AI members revealed a high degree of internal cohesion on the same belief system, which is considered a relevant source of influence (Cross, 2013). This circumstance is not merely the effect of the selection procedure of the expert group. The recent Berkman Klein Center's (2020) review of AI ethics and governance guidelines highlighted a high degree of convergence on the same set of principles and procedures, which seems to indicate the existence of a broad transnational epistemic community engaged with AI ethics. After all, the EC "Artificial Intelligence for Europe" strategy that established the HLEG-AI is itself the result of a previous intense dialogue with the European Group on Ethics in Science and New Technologies.

These final observations suggest that the capability of epistemic communities to produce consistent, evidence-based, and intersubjectively validated normative frameworks draws the attention of decision-makers and inform their views. This article suggest that such a kind of epistemic community has emerged in the AI ethics field and it is promoting an approach grounded on fundamental human rights. However, further research is needed to explore how and to what extent this framework will be embedded within companies' operational and organizational practices, as well as if competing epistemic communities are emerging from different cultural and geopolitical backgrounds.
