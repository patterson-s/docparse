The interesting part of the article by KSS is that it links power (hegemony) to norm making through expertise. The global health case shows that expertise is mobilized in the shadow of power (or hegemony). 

  

I downloaded “Global Health Expertise in the Shadow of Hegemony” [(Kentikelenis, Seabrooke, and Sending 2023)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DrbizY). I am just starting to read it, but I think that it is a great point of departure for investigating AI expertise. 

  

For a few reasons. 

- Their research question: “What enables actors to shape norms in global health governance?” 
    
- Their answer: “norm-making in global health governance occurs in the shadow of hegemony; a system of status and stratification that is centered on economic and security concerns and maintained by countries at the core of the world system” (pg. 347)
    

  

The research question would be interesting to reframe for global AI governance

- What enables actors to shape norms in global AI governance? 
    

  

At the same time, there is another wrinkle. The KSS article is published in 2023; the authors argue that health governance is in the shadow of hegemony. I think that hegemony is no longer something that we can take for granted - in any case, the structure of AI development cannot be assumed to be hegemonic. AI development produces and takes place within a system of stratification - but a less articulated one than hegemony. 

  

One quote on page 348: 

- “At heart, the distribution of power in the world system, and the epistemic authority on which global health measures are based, bring out two conflicting principles for policy making: Claims to scientific authority are often cast as universal, transcending national borders. The logic of international politics, by contrast, suggests that particular (state) interests prevail, trumping claims to universal scientific authority. Explaining the contents and changes of global health by reference to transnational health expertise, then, risks ignoring the systemic-level political context of hegemonic powers.”
    

  

How might we think about this with respect to AI? 

- Claims to scientific authority in AI have less time; the WHO goes back a long time. 
    
- Novelty also carries some weight in terms of diffusion of expertise; there aren’t legacy institutions - a new period of institutionalization is taking place. 
    
- AI development is tied to a peculiar material and financing system. This creates high levels of asymmetry. 
    

  

In AI, who is asserting claims to universal scientific authority? 

- Perhaps better, a focus on AI safety. Challenge the notion of universal AI safety - focus instead on differential AI safety, with a more expansive view.
